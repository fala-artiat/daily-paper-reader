# Optimal Codes for the Coverage Depth Problem and the Performance of Random Codes 

R. Gross ∗, Y. Grunbaum ∗, M. Bertuzzo †, E. Yaakobi ∗, A. Ravagnani †∗ Department of Computer Science, Technion—Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa 3200003, Israel {roee.g, yitzchakg, yaakobi}@cs.technion.ac.il

Department of Mathematics and Computer Science, Eindhoven University of Technology, the Netherlands {m.bertuzzo, a.ravagnani}@tue.nl 

Abstract 

DNA storage systems retrieve information by randomly sampling synthesized DNA strands, making the number of reads required for successful recovery a fundamental performance measure. This motivates the coverage depth problem: for given code parameters, determine a linear code that minimizes the expected number of randomly sampled columns required to span the entire information space. While MDS codes are known to be optimal whenever they exist, identifying optimal codes in parameter regimes where MDS codes do not exist remains largely open. In this work, we derive a general formula for the expectation in the coverage depth setting, and apply this result to establish the optimality of the 𝑞 -ary simplex code for the parameters that allow its existence. Moreover, we show the optimality of the 𝑞 -ary Hamming code by relating coverage depth to independent sets in the dual code and exploiting log-concavity properties of matroid basis-generating polynomials. We further analyze random linear codes, derive an exact expression for their expected coverage depth, and show that, in the constant-rate regime, their additive gap from the MDS benchmark remains bounded independently of the code length. 

I. Introduction DNA-based storage systems represent a promising solution for addressing the unprecedented growth of digital information that needs to be stored globally [1]. Extreme compactness (orders of magnitude denser than any traditional storage medium), durability under suitable conditions, and low maintenance costs [2], [3] are distinctive characteristics that make DNA-based systems particularly attractive for long-term data storage. The DNA storage pipeline involves multiple steps: After encoding data into sequences over the DNA alphabet {𝐴, 𝐶, 𝐺,𝑇 } and dividing them into blocks, these blocks are synthesized into artificial DNA molecules known as strands . Current limitations restrict the length of the synthesized strands [4]; fur-thermore, copies of each strand are produced that may contain errors. The resulting molecules are then stored in a container in an unordered manner. The process required to retrieve the stored information is sequencing, during which the strands are translated into digital sequences, called reads , which are noisy copies of the stored strands. The reads are then decoded back into the original information. A key feature of this process is that the DNA sequences are randomly read to decode the original information. Although various works have demonstrated the potential of DNA as a storage medium [5]–[11], critical issues related to the efficiency of DNA sequencers [2], [12], [13] remain, resulting in slow throughput and high costs compared to other storage technologies. This bottleneck is closely related to the notion of coverage depth [14], defined as the ratio between the number of reads that are sequenced and the number of designed DNA strands. Thus, an opportunity to improve efficiency and reduce costs consists of reducing the coverage depth by reducing the number of reads needed for successful recovery. arXiv:2610.07370v1 [cs.IT] 5 Oct 2026 2

In a recent paper [15], various problems aimed at enhancing the reliability and effectiveness of data retrieval were introduced and analyzed for the first time, with special emphasis on the random access coverage depth problem ; in this model, the user’s data is represented by 𝑘 information strands, which are encoded into 𝑛 strands through a matrix 𝐺 ∈ F𝑘 ×𝑛 𝑞 , typically referred to as a generator matrix of an error-correcting code. The goal is to compute, and possibly minimize, the expected number of columns to draw to recover a specific information strand among the 𝑘 that have been stored. A similar version is represented by the DNA coverage depth problem , where all the 𝑘 information strands must be retrieved. Both problems have been studied primarily under the assumptions of error-free synthesis and sequencing, and uniform distribution when drawing the columns of the matrix. The random access model has attracted growing interest in different directions, such as identifying the structural properties of generator matrices that are advantageous [16], providing optimal constructions for specific cases [17], [18], and extending this model at the file level [19]. Furthermore, [20], [21] improved upon these constructions, deriving bounds and computing asymptotic performances, while [22] focused on the optimization problem in an asymptotic regime in the binary case. On the other hand, the DNA coverage depth problem was addressed in [15], where the authors proved that MDS codes achieve the best result in minimizing the expected number of reads by drawing connections to the classical coupon collector’s problem [23]–[26]. Moreover, in [27], real DNA storage experimental data were used to extend the problem to noisy channels with log-normal distribution. Building on the framework presented in [15], the DNA coverage depth problem was studied in [28], [29] in regimes where MDS codes do not exist. More specifically, closed-form expressions were derived for the expected value of columns for 𝑞 -ary simplex codes, Hamming codes, first-order Reed-Muller codes, and also for ternary Golay codes. In the first part of this paper, we address an open problem presented in [29]: Our goal is to identify codes that minimize the expected number of reads required to retrieve all the information (i.e., optimal codes) for specific choices of parameters, none of which admits the existence of MDS codes. More specifically, we establish the optimality of various code families: 𝑞 -ary simplex codes, first-order 𝑞 -ary Reed-Muller codes, and 𝑞 -ary Hamming codes. Concurrently with the preparation of this manuscript, Kong et al. [30] independently established the same optimality result for the 𝑞 -ary simplex code in the coverage depth problem. The second part is inspired by a recent paper by Wang and Yaakobi [21], where random matrices were used to obtain bounds on the optimal coverage depth, showing in particular that, at constant rate, its additive gap from the MDS bound remains bounded. In this work, we study the random-code ensemble itself and derive an exact expression for its average coverage depth, which enables us to tighten the upper bound on the additive gap from the MDS coverage time by a factor of two compared to the bound established in [21]. While [21] established that the gap is linear in 𝑛 within the constant redundancy regime, we demonstrate that our approach yields the same result. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we introduce the notation and definitions used throughout the paper. Section III presents a general formula for the expectation and an application of it to the first-order 𝑞 -ary Reed-Muller codes. Section IV, Section V, and Section VI establish the optimality of the 𝑞 -ary Simplex code, the first-order 𝑞 -ary Reed-Muller code, and the 𝑞 -ary Hamming code, respectively. Finally, in Section VII, we analyze the coverage depth of random linear codes. II. A primary motivation for the study of coverage depth comes from DNA-based data storage. During DNA readout, individual molecules are sampled from a pool, and direct random access to the encoded symbols is generally unavailable. As a result, recovery requires collecting samples until they jointly provide sufficient information to reconstruct the stored data. From a coding-theoretic perspective, it is therefore natural to seek codes that minimize the expected number of samples required for recovery. VIII. Summary and Open Problems In this work, we studied the coverage depth of several important families of linear codes. While MDS codes are known to be optimal with respect to coverage depth whenever they exist, we showed that the 

𝑞 -ary Simplex and Hamming codes are also optimal for their respective parameters. We also analyzed the coverage depth of random linear codes and showed that, in the constant-rate regime, their coverage depth approaches the MDS bound. Nevertheless, determining the coverage depth, and in particular establishing optimality, for many other important families of codes remains open. A natural direction for future work is therefore to characterize the coverage depth of additional structured code families and to identify further parameter regimes in which optimal codes can be determined. More generally, it would be interesting to obtain structural conditions that characterize, or even restrict, the class of codes that are optimal with respect to coverage depth. IX.
