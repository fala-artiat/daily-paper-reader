# The Coverage Depth Problem in Distributed DNA Data Storage 

Xiangliang Kong, Ohad Elishco, Chen Wang, and Tolga M. Duman 

Abstract 

Random sampling in DNA sequencing produces repeated reads, increasing retrieval latency and sequencing cost. We study the coverage-depth problem for full-message recovery in distributed DNA storage under noiseless uniform sampling, where strands are partitioned among M containers and one strand is independently sampled with replacement from each container per round. For arbitrary linear codes and ordered partitions, we derive exact formulas for the recovery-time distribution and expectation. We prove that MDS codes, whenever they exist, are optimal for every fixed partition, and establish a universal lower bound on the expected total read cost together with its equality conditions. For MDS codes, we identify container-size regimes that yield genuine savings in total reads and regimes that provide only parallelism without changing the asymptotic sequencing cost. For simplex codes, we prove that the q-ary simplex code is, up to isomorphism, the unique single-container minimizer among codes with the same parameters, resolving a recent conjecture by Bertuzzo, Ravagnani, and Yaakobi. We further construct a partition attaining the minimum total read cost and derive bounds for intermediate and balanced partitions. These results clarify when distributed sampling reduces latency alone and when it also reduces sequencing cost. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

DNA-based data storage has emerged as a promising technology for long-term digital archiving. DNA offers extremely high information density, long-term chemical stability, and low maintenance requirements, and a number of proof-of-concept systems have demonstrated the feasibility of storing and retrieving digital information using synthetic DNA molecules [1]–[4]. The coding-theoretic and information-theoretic challenges arising in such systems have consequently attracted much attention, for example, see the surveys [5], [6] and the references therein. A typical DNA storage pipeline consists of encoding, synthesis, physical storage, sequencing, and decoding. The user data are first encoded into a collection of short DNA sequences, commonly referred to as oligos or strands .These designed strands are synthesized, amplified into many physical copies, and stored together in one or more containers. To retrieve the information, DNA molecules are sampled and sequenced, producing an unordered multiset of reads. Indices embedded in the strands may identify the designed strand from which a read originates, but they do not make the molecules directly addressable: the sequencing process still samples molecules from the physical pool, and repeated observations of the same designed strand are unavoidable. This random-sampling feature distinguishes DNA storage from conventional storage devices. Even in an idealized noiseless model, a sequencer may repeatedly return strands that have already been observed, while other strands remain unseen. Consequently, the number of reads required for decoding can be substantially larger than the number of information strands from the message. In physical terms, the coverage depth is the number of sequenced reads normalized by the number of designed strands [7]. Since the number of designed strands is fixed for a given code, minimizing coverage depth is equivalent to minimizing the total number of reads. The latter is directly related to both sequencing cost and recovery latency. The resulting sampling problem is closely connected to the classical coupon collector problem [8]–[11]. In the uncoded setting, every designed strand is a coupon type, and decoding requires observing all coupon types. More precisely, consider a k-dimensional linear code C ⊆ Fnq with a rank-k generator matrix G. Each column of G corresponds to one coded DNA strand. In the single-container noiseless model, a read selects one column uniformly at random with replacement, and the message can be fully recovered when the sampled columns span Fkq .Bar-Lev et al. [12] initiated the systematic theoretical study of this stopping time, referred to as the coverage-depth problem in DNA data storage . They investigated its dependence on the sampling distribution, the outer code, and the strand-retrieval procedure. In particular, for uniform noiseless sampling, they proved that an [n, k ]q MDS code, whenever it exists, minimizes the expected number of reads among [n, k ]q linear codes. 

A. Related Work 

Several extensions of the coverage-depth problem in DNA data storage have recently been investigated. Besides full-message recovery, Bar-Lev et al. [12] introduced the singleton random-access problem, in which the user requests one prescribed information strand rather than the entire message. The combinatorial and geometric aspects of random access have subsequently been studied in [13]–[15], which showed that code optimality for full-message recovery does not imply optimality for random-access objectives. Other related models include recovering groups of strands representing a common information unit [16], opti-mizing the trade-off between synthesis and sequencing costs through practical coding architectures [17], analyzing sequencing coverage in combinatorial shortmer-based storage systems [18], and studying coverage and decoding probabilities for composite DNA letters [19]. These models modify either the physical representation of information or the decoding objective, and therefore lead to stopping rules different from the full-rank condition considered in this paper. When MDS codes do not exist over the prescribed field, the performance of structured small-field codes becomes a central question. Bertuzzo, Ravagnani, and Yaakobi [20] developed algebraic and combinatorial methods for computing the single-container coverage depth through information-set enumeration, duality and weight distributions. They obtained explicit formulas for several classical code families, including simplex, Hamming, ternary Golay, extended ternary Golay, and first-order Reed–Muller codes. Based on the resulting formula for simplex codes and computational evidence, they conjectured that the q-ary simplex code minimizes the coverage depth among all codes having the same length and dimension. One of the results of the present paper resolves this conjecture in the affirmative and establishes the corresponding uniqueness statement. 

B. Distributed Full-Message Recovery 

Physical separation of coded data across multiple storage units is a standard mechanism for providing parallel access and fault tolerance. In classical distributed storage systems, a file is encoded across several nodes, and a central question is how much information must be downloaded from the surviving nodes to repair a failed one. The repair-bandwidth problem was introduced by Dimakis et al. [21] and has since developed into an extensive theory of regenerating and related storage codes; see [22] for a survey. DNA containers, however, are passive physical objects rather than computational nodes. A container cannot evaluate a prescribed function of its contents and transmit the result. Instead, information is obtained by sequencing, and every read returns a randomly sampled strand from the container. Thus, even if the same erasure code is used, the download process in DNA storage is fundamentally different from that in a conventional distributed storage system. Levy, Con, Yaakobi, and Kiah [23] recently initiated the study of DNA-based distributed storage from the viewpoint of container failures. Their goal is to reconstruct the contents of a failed container by sequencing the surviving containers, and they analyzed the associated recovery time for scalar MDS and MDS array codes. The objective of the present paper is complementary. We consider the problem of recovering the entire message 

when the coded strands are distributed among several containers. This question arises even in the absence of container failures and remains necessary after a failed container has been repaired: the ultimate readout operation must still accumulate enough information to decode the complete message. The two problems also have different stopping rules. Failed-container recovery asks whether a prescribed missing block is determined by the observations from the surviving containers, whereas full-message recovery asks whether all observed coded strands jointly span the entire k-dimensional message space. 3

Specifically, we partition the n columns of G into M labeled containers according to an ordered partition P. In every sequencing round, one column is sampled independently and uniformly, with replacement across rounds, from each container. Let τ (G, P) be the first round in which the sampled columns span Fkq . Since parallel sequencing can reduce latency without increasing the total number of reads, we distinguish two performance metrics and consider them separately: the expected recovery time in rounds, E[τ (G, P)] , and the expected total number of reads, M E[τ (G, P)] .This formulation gives rise to the distributed coverage depth problem , which consists of the following three interconnected questions: (1) For a fixed ordered M -partition, which code minimizes the expected recovery time? (2) For a fixed code and fixed M , how to partition its columns to achieve the smallest expected recovery time? (3) Under practical capacity constraints (such as balanced container sizes), which codes have a genuine reduction in the total sequencing cost (total number of reads), rather than only a reduction in latency (measured in recovery rounds)? In this paper, we address the three questions listed above. We study the expected recovery time for both general linear codes and specific code families, providing explicit estimations of the expected recovery rounds. Furthermore, we analyze the extent to which single-container overhead can be reduced in distributed settings. 

C. Main Contributions 

The principal contributions of this work are summarized below.  

> •

By decomposing the recovery process into successive stages and using q-binomial M¨ obius inversion on the subspaces of Fkq , we derive an exact formula for E[τ (G, P)] for an arbitrary rank-k linear code with generator matrix G and an arbitrary ordered partition P. 

> •

We show that whenever an [n, k ]q MDS code exists, it is optimal for every fixed partition, both in terms of expected recovery time and total read cost. Furthermore, we establish a general lower bound on the expected number of recovery rounds, hence on the expected total number of reads, and we characterize exactly when 

τ (G, P) = 1 a.s. In particular, for M = k the bound is attained by the staircase partition of any linear code, so that min M, P M E[τ (G, P)] = k for every [n, k ]q code. We then exhibit one-round-perfect layouts with additional structure for MDS, Hamming, first-order Reed-Muller and simplex codes.  

> •

We consider the distributed coverage depth problem for MDS codes. When every container has a fixed size s,we identify explicit parameter regimes in which the recovery time concentrates on an integer r. Within part of this regime, the resulting total read cost is lower than that of the single-container case by a quantity linear in k. For containers of size kε with 0 < ε < 1, we derive asymptotic formulas for both the expected recovery time and the total number of reads, showing that distribution provides parallel access without changing the asymptotic sequencing cost.  

> •

We also consider the distributed coverage depth problem for simplex codes. We first show that the q-ary simplex code is, up to isomorphism, the unique length-[k]q , dimension-k code that minimizes the single-container coverage depth, thereby resolving [20, Conjecture 3.2]. Then, we provide an explicit k-partition that achieves the optimal total number of reads for recovery. Moreover, for 1 < M < k we show that an intermediate number of containers cannot reduce the total read cost whenever M ∤ k and M ∤ k + 1 , and also whenever M | k + 1 provided q ⩾ 3 or k = 3 . The remaining regimes, namely M | k for every q, and 

M | k + 1 for q = 2 and k ⩾ 4, are settled for cases (q, k, M ) = (2 , 5, 2) and (3 , 4, 2) .Taken together, these results reveal that distributed sampling has two fundamentally distinct effects: parallelism reduces recovery latency, whereas a reduction in total sequencing cost requires the layout (G, P) to suppress repeated or rank-deficient observations. The former effect is largely architectural; the latter depends delicately on the interaction between the algebraic properties of the code and the combinatorial structure of the partition. 

D. Organization 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II introduces the distributed coverage depth model along with its metrics and foundational notions; it derives exact formulas for E[τ (G, P)] , proves the optimality of MDS codes, and develops general lower bounds on the total number of reads and the theory of one-round-perfect layouts. Section III specializes this framework to MDS codes and investigates the associated distributed coupon-collector problem in constant-size, polynomial-size, and two-container regimes. Section IV focuses on simplex codes, establishing their unique single-container optimality and analyzing staircase, balanced, and random balanced layouts. Finally, Section V concludes the paper and outlines several open questions. 4

C. The Optimality of MDS Codes in Distributed Model 

In [12], Bar-Lev et al. studied the coverage depth problem for general codes and proved that MDS codes are optimal in terms of minimizing the expected number of reads among all codes with the same parameters. Next, we show that MDS codes are also optimal in terms of the expected number of recovery rounds in the distributed storage model. 

Lemma II.5. Let G = ( g1, . . . , gn), eG = ( eg1, . . . , egn) ∈ Fk×nq be two different rank-k generator matrices, and let P = {P1, . . . , P M } be any fixed M -partition of [n]. Suppose that 

rank( G|J ) ⩾ rank( eG|J ), ∀ J ⊆ [n]. (12)

D ISTRIBUTED COVERAGE DEPTH PROBLEM OF SIMPLEX CODES 

In this section, we consider the distributed coverage depth problem of simplex codes. We first study the general coverage depth problem in the single-container model. As a consequence, we show that the simplex code is optimal in terms of coverage depth in the single-container model, which resolves a conjecture proposed by Bertuzzo, Ravagnani, and Yaakobi [20]. Next, we consider the distributed setting and show that the simplex code achieves optimal distributed coverage depth when the partition P has a staircase form. Furthermore, we obtain the asymptotic formula for the distributed coverage depth of the simplex code when the partition P is randomly balanced. Throughout this section, we use Gsimp to denote the standard normalized generator matrix of simplex code, whose columns are the representatives of the projective points with first nonzero coordinate equal to 1.

V. C ONCLUSION AND OPEN PROBLEMS 

This paper establishes a general framework for analyzing full-message recovery in distributed DNA storage via the notion of distributed coverage depth. First, we provide exact formulas, general lower bounds, and optimal layout constructions for linear codes, proving that MDS codes remain optimal in minimizing the expected number of sequencing rounds. Second, a systematic evaluation of MDS codes reveals a key trade-off: while constant-size balanced containers achieve a genuine reduction in total reads compared to the single-container case, polynomial-size containers merely accelerate recovery via parallelism without reducing the total read volume. Furthermore, our low-rate lower bounds and higher-rate constructions show that these distributional gains are governed jointly by code rate and container scaling. Third, for simplex codes, we resolve a conjecture by Bertuzzo, Ravagnani, and Yaakobi [20] in the single-container setting, construct optimal staircase layouts achieving minimal total reads when M = k, and bound the total-read overhead for both deterministic and random balanced layouts. Together, these results demonstrate that distribution is neither inherently beneficial nor universally detrimental. Ultimately, whether distribution reduces coverage overhead or merely provides parallel speedup depends fundamen-tally on the underlying rank geometry of the code and the combinatorial structure of the layout. Several natural directions and open problems emerge from our results. Here we highlight a few of them for future research. 1) Optimal layouts under given constraints. Even when the partition is required to be balanced, a structural characterization of the optimal layout is not known for a general code. It would be particularly useful to characterize which codes admit balanced one-round-perfect layouts and, when this is impossible, to determine the smallest achievable number of total reads. A related question is to develop nearly balanced constructions when M ∤ n.2) Optimal codes over small fields. Given a field Fq , a fixed integer M , and a fixed ordered M -partition P of

While this problem is completely settled when MDS codes exist, it remains open for parameters where no MDS code is available (e.g., over small fields where q < n − 3). In the single-container setting, the analogous question was studied in [20] using duality arguments and extended weight enumerators; adapting such combinatorial tools may provide a fruitful direction for the distributed storage model. 3) Simplex code layouts with intermediate numbers of containers. The integrality arguments rule out savings for many values of q, k, and M , and the codimension-two analysis settles two small exceptional cases. It remains to determine the optimal distributed coverage depth in the remaining regimes, especially when M | k, and in the binary cases where M | (k + 1) .4) Nonuniform, asynchronous, adaptive, and noisy sampling. The present model assumes that all strands within a container are equally likely to be sampled, that one read is obtained from every container in every round, and that every sampled strand is observed without error. Practical systems may exhibit unequal strand abundances, different sequencing rates across containers, adaptive allocation of reads, erasures, or sequencing errors. Extending the present results to these models, and determining which of the layout-optimality principles remain valid, are natural directions for future work.
